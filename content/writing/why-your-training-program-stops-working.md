You have been training and logging your workouts, and for a while the numbers moved. Now the same weights feel harder and progress has slowed, or the results you expected never showed up.

Before you change the whole program, find out what actually stopped working. A missed expectation, inconsistent execution, and a real programming problem each call for a different response.

My troubleshooting process starts with the goal and the plan built to reach it. Then I check whether that plan has been followed. Only after those questions are answered do I look at the training stimulus and whether the person can recover from it. Exercise changes come later, unless safety or suitability calls for an adjustment right away.

## Recheck the goal and the roadmap

"I want to get stronger" gives us a direction, but not enough information to evaluate a program. Stronger at what, over what period, and with how many sessions available each week? A beginner building general strength and an experienced lifter preparing for a specific performance need different expectations.

The same applies to changing your physique. Decide what you are measuring and how you will compare it. If your goal is body composition, one workout's performance cannot answer the question.

Then look back at the original roadmap. What was supposed to improve during this block, and was the timeframe realistic? Check whether the plan still fits your schedule and circumstances.

A useful goal ties an outcome to behaviors you can repeat, so we can evaluate both the result and the process that was supposed to produce it.

## Check what was actually completed

A program written for three sessions a week cannot be judged as if all three happened when attendance has averaged one or two.

Missing sessions does not make someone lazy. It does mean they got less training than the plan assumed, and we need to find out why.

Start with the record:

- How many planned sessions did you complete?
- Did you perform the prescribed working sets and repetitions?
- Did your effort match the instructions?
- Were the agreed nutrition and activity habits followed?
- Did you log enough information to see a pattern?

Effort matters, but it needs context. Feeling exhausted at the end of a session does not tell us whether you completed the work the program called for.

If adherence is inconsistent, work on the reason. A reliable two-day plan may fit someone's life better than a four-day plan they keep missing, and the goal stays the same.

The 2026 American College of Sports Medicine guidance emphasizes regular participation and individualizing training around goals, preferences, and sustainability. That supports checking consistency before adding complexity.[1]

Coaches have responsibility here, too. If a client repeatedly cannot follow the plan, investigate the schedule, instructions, support, and demands of the program. Accountability includes building a system the person can realistically execute.

## Judge progress against the current training phase

Not every block is trying to improve the same thing.

In my programming approach, an **intensification phase** emphasizes heavier loading and strength expression, and an **accumulation phase** emphasizes building training volume and work capacity. The progress you look for should match that emphasis.

During accumulation, completing more quality repetitions or handling the planned workload may be progress even if the weight on the bar stays the same. During intensification, the loading trend matters more, as long as execution holds up.

Judging each block by its own emphasis keeps us from calling it unsuccessful for missing a result it was never designed to prioritize.

Research supports matching training variables to the adaptation you want. The 2026 ACSM review found that heavier loads can enhance strength outcomes and higher training volumes can enhance muscle growth. It did not find that complex periodization consistently improved outcomes across the healthy-adult evidence it reviewed.[1]

I use phases to organize decisions and manage how demanding the training is. Someone making steady progress on a simple program does not need extra complexity just to make the plan look advanced.

## When loading stalls, check readiness

If a normally manageable weight has become unusually hard, look at what changed around the training. Ask about sleep, stress, fatigue, soreness, and how the person has recovered since the last session, and review the recent workload as well. A program that fit someone's capacity last month may need adjusting during a demanding stretch at work or at home.

One poor session is worth watching. A run of declining sessions deserves a closer review.

When an intensification block stalls, my first adjustment is often to cut unnecessary volume while keeping the main objective. Depending on the situation, I may also allow more rest, or swap a demanding variation for one the person can perform better that day. Each of those changes should have a reason I can explain and a result I can watch for.

Persistent or unexplained pain needs a different response. Stop the activity that provokes it and seek appropriate clinical guidance when needed. A training adjustment does not establish a diagnosis, and a trainer should not treat it as one.

## If the plan is being followed, examine the program

Once the goal is realistic and adherence is established, the coach needs to look hard at the prescription: whether the training is a suitable challenge, whether the progression rules are clear, whether the workload is recoverable, and how execution and the measurements used to judge the result hold up.

If the client is doing what was agreed and progress remains absent, repeatedly asking for more discipline avoids the programming question. The coach is responsible for investigating and adjusting the regimen within their scope.

Nobody can promise results on a fixed schedule, but a coach can take ownership of the decisions they control.

Make the smallest change likely to address the limitation you found. If you change frequency, exercises, repetitions, loading, and nutrition all at once, it becomes much harder to tell what helped. Say why you are making the change, decide what improvement would look like, and give the new approach enough time to be evaluated.

## Decide whether the phase has run its course

In my system, an intensification phase is deliberately limited. I do not keep pushing heavier loading when performance is declining, joint discomfort is building, or the person's willingness to train is slipping.

In that context, roughly two weeks of stalled loading can prompt a review. That is not a universal deadline for declaring a plateau, and two difficult sessions are not a reason to switch programs.

The next step may be a different phase, a deload, or a period of consolidation.

A **deload** reduces training stress. After heavy loading I may reduce load; after a demanding volume block I may reduce volume. The adjustment depends on what needs to come down.

A **consolidation phase**, as I use the term, aims to hold on to recent gains while easing off the push to keep progressing. I may use two to four weeks, depending on the person and the block before it. It is not meant to remove every source of fatigue.

A 2023 expert-consensus study offers a framework for individualized deloading and acknowledges that the best approach is still under-researched.[2] Plan a deload with a purpose and a follow-up assessment, and do not count on it to reset everything by itself.

## Change exercises for a reason

When the current exercises are safe, suitable, and still able to progress, exercise selection belongs farther down the troubleshooting sequence. If an exercise still fits the goal, can be performed well, and allows appropriate loading, it may not need replacing. Familiarity alone is a weak reason to drop it.

When a movement is not going well, find the limitation. A short cue, a setup adjustment, added support, a range modification, or a lower load may solve it. If the load clearly exceeds control, reduce it immediately. If the movement is still unsuitable, choose another exercise.

This is part of the JR Coaching Standard: observe the person, identify what needs intervention, and adapt the plan deliberately. The exercise has to serve the person's training objective, and they have to be able to perform it safely and repeatably.

## Record whether the change worked

After an adjustment, record what changed and why. Name the outcome you expect to improve, then compare relevant sessions or measurements under reasonably consistent conditions.

For strength, that might be load and repetitions with comparable execution. For an accumulation block, it might be completing the planned workload without the drop in performance you saw before. Pick the measure that answers your question, and judge the pattern and its context rather than one exceptional or disappointing day.

My Intervention Hierarchy asks coaches to establish sufficient fundamentals, individualize where it meaningfully helps, and add advanced methods when their benefit justifies the cost. That principle also applies to troubleshooting: solve the identified problem with an intervention you can evaluate.

Before you replace your program, answer these questions:

1. Is the goal clear and the timeline realistic?
2. Have you consistently completed the agreed plan?
3. Are you measuring the outcome this phase is meant to develop?
4. Does the workload fit your current recovery capacity?
5. What specific change addresses the limitation you found?
