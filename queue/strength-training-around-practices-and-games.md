Strength training should make your kid better at their sport. If it leaves them too sore to practice or flat on game day, the schedule needs fixing. The lifting itself is usually fine.

## Lifting belongs in the plan

The 2014 international consensus statement, endorsed by sports medicine and pediatric organizations, supports resistance training for children and adolescents when it's properly designed and supervised. It improves strength and movement skill and can help reduce sports injury risk.[1]

The key words are designed and supervised. A young athlete needs a plan that fits their season, and a coach watching technique.

## Off-season: build

The off-season is where most of the strength gets built. Practices are lighter or gone, so there's room for two or three lifting sessions a week, more total work and new skills in the weight room.

This is also the time to learn the main movements well. An athlete who learns to squat, hinge, push and pull properly in the off-season has something solid to carry into the season.

## Preseason: sharpen

As practices ramp up, lifting volume should come down. The sport is now adding a lot of running, jumping and contact, and all of it counts as load. Keep the main lifts, keep the intensity honest, and do less total work.

## In-season: maintain

In season, the goal is to hold what was built. That takes less than most people think.

In one study, professional soccer players who did one strength session a week during the first 12 weeks of their season kept the strength and sprint gains from their preseason. A group that lifted only every other week lost some strength and sprint speed.[2] Those were adult professionals, so apply it to a high schooler with some care. The idea still holds: one or two short, focused sessions a week can keep an athlete strong through a season.

## Where sessions go in the week

**Keep heavy lower-body work away from game day.** The day before a game is a bad day for hard squats or jumps.

**Use the days after games for lighter work.** A shorter session with upper-body lifts and easy movement can fit here, depending on how the athlete feels.

**Put the hardest session furthest from the next game.** If there's a game Saturday, early in the week is usually the place for it.

**When practice and lifting fall on the same day, lift after practice or with plenty of time between.** Skill work goes better when the athlete is fresh.

Every schedule is different, and a lot of athletes are on more than one team. The plan has to be built around the week your athlete actually has.

## Count the whole load

Practices, games, club teams, PE, a growth spurt, finals week and short sleep all add up. When an athlete starts looking tired, sore all the time or slower than usual, look at the whole week before changing anything in the weight room.

## Questions to ask your athlete's coach

- How does the plan change from off-season to in-season?
- How many lifting sessions a week right now, and why that number?
- What happens to lifting during a week with two games?
- Who watches technique during sessions?
