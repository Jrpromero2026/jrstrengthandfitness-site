A lot of trainers run a thorough assessment and then write a program that looks exactly like the one they'd write for anyone. The client spent an hour being tested, and nothing in their plan shows it.

If an assessment doesn't change a decision, it didn't do its job. The point of assessing a client is to make better programming decisions faster. Here's how I'd connect the two.

## Know what you're deciding before you test

Before the first test, be clear on the decisions the assessment is supposed to inform. For most new clients it comes down to five:

1. What's the priority for the first block?
2. Which exercises fit this person right now?
3. Where do the starting loads go?
4. How much total work can they handle?
5. What needs watching or a referral?

Every part of your assessment should feed at least one of those. If a test doesn't, drop it. You'll have a shorter assessment and a client who doesn't feel like they're being processed.

## The conversation comes first

The most useful part of any assessment is the conversation. Goals, training history, what's gotten in the way before, injuries, schedule, and what the client actually enjoys. I covered the questions that run that conversation in [5 Questions to Ask During a Personal Training Consultation](/notes/5-questions-to-ask-during-a-personal-training-consultation).

That conversation answers decision one before you've tested anything. A client with three available days, a desk job and a goal of feeling strong again needs a different first block than a former athlete with five days and a specific performance target, no matter what their movement looks like.

## Map findings to decisions

Once you know the priority, use what you see to make the other calls.

**Movement and positions decide exercise selection.** If a client can't get into a comfortable, controlled position on a movement, that tells you what to start with and what to build toward. It doesn't tell you the client is broken. Pick the variation they can own today, and plan the progression from there.

**Early sessions decide starting loads.** I'd rather find working weights by building up across the first sessions than guess from a formula. Start lighter than you think, watch technique and effort, and let the first couple of weeks show you where they really are.

**History and recovery decide volume.** Someone who hasn't trained in years, sleeps six hours and works long shifts can't recover from the same volume as a trained 25-year-old. Start with the least work that will drive progress and add from there.

**Red flags decide referrals.** Pain that changes with movement, symptoms that don't fit, or anything outside your scope goes to the right professional. An assessment can't diagnose anything, and a trainer shouldn't try to.

## Don't over-read what you find

The more common mistake is reading too much into a single test.

One awkward squat on day one tells you the squat needs coaching. It doesn't tell you there's a hidden dysfunction that needs a month of corrective work before the client is allowed to lift. Most early movement problems clean up with good coaching, the right variation and practice.

This is where my intervention hierarchy comes in: Inadequate, Adequate, Optimal and Enhanced. Use the lowest level of complexity that reliably works. Complexity is earned by the level beneath it. A new client who needs a safe, simple plan and a reason to keep showing up doesn't need a ten-point corrective program. Most coaches over-intervene, and the client pays for it in boredom and slow progress.

## Treat the first block as part of the assessment

The assessment doesn't end on day one. The first few weeks of training tell you more than any test: how the client responds to load, how they recover, whether they do the work between sessions, and what they enjoy.

The first five sessions decide whether a client stays. Don't spend them trying to impress anyone with intensity. Spend them finding out who you're coaching, getting some early wins, and adjusting the plan as you learn.

## Write the decisions down

For every new client, write a short record: what you found, what you decided, and why. "Starting with a box squat because they couldn't control depth on a free squat. Retest at week four." It takes a few minutes and it keeps your programming honest. It also means that when you retest, you're comparing against a decision instead of a vague memory.

An assessment is only as good as the programming it produces. Decide what you need to know, test for that, and make sure the client can see the results in their plan.
