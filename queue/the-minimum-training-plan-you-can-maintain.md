Most training plans are built for your best week. A minimum plan is built for your worst one: the week with the sick kid, the deadline and four hours of sleep.

Everyone should have one. When life gets busy, people with a minimum plan keep training. People without one stop, and then they have to start over.

## What a minimum plan is

It's the smallest amount of training that still holds your progress, or moves it a little, that you'd do even in a bad week. You write it ahead of time, so you don't have to make decisions when you're tired.

I use an intervention hierarchy in my coaching: Inadequate, Adequate, Optimal and Enhanced. A minimum plan lives at Adequate, and Adequate is a legitimate target, not a failure. Plenty of people would get better results from a plan they keep doing than from an optimal plan they keep abandoning.

## What the research says it takes

Less than you'd guess.

A 2021 review on maintaining performance found that strength and muscle size, at least in younger people, can be held for up to 32 weeks with as little as one strength session a week and one set per exercise, as long as the intensity stays up.[1] That's for holding what you have, not building more.

If you want to keep making progress on limited time, a 2021 review on time-efficient training recommends building sessions around multi-joint exercises that work both sides of the body, and notes that meaningful gains can come from low training volumes.[2] For general health, the CDC recommends muscle-strengthening activity at least two days a week.[3]

Put together: two days a week of a few big lifts, done with real effort, covers most people.

## How to build yours

**Pick two days you can protect.** Look at your hardest weeks, not your average ones. Choose the two days that survive even those.

**Choose four to six movements.** A squat or leg press, a hinge like a Romanian deadlift, a push, a pull, and a carry or core exercise if there's time. Big movements give you the most for the time you have.

**Keep the effort real.** Fewer sets is fine. Easy sets aren't. The research above holds up only when intensity stays high, so each set should end close to the point where your form would start to slip.

**Cap the time.** Thirty to forty minutes, including a short warm-up specific to the first lift. If the plan needs an hour, it's not your minimum.

**Write down two versions.** The minimum plan, and the full plan you run when life allows. Then you're never deciding from scratch. You're just choosing which version this week gets.

## What one can look like

Here's an example. Swap exercises for whatever your gym has:

**Day 1:** goblet squat, dumbbell bench press, one-arm row, plank. Two or three sets each.

**Day 2:** Romanian deadlift, split squat, overhead press, lat pulldown, farmer carry. Two or three sets each.

That's it. If you get a third day, add one. If you only get one day, do Day 1 and call the week a success.

## When to use it

December is the obvious one. So is travel, a move, a new baby, a big project at work or any week that's already full. If you know a rough stretch is coming, switch to the minimum plan before it starts.
