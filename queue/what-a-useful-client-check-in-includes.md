Most client check-ins are a form nobody reads closely. "How was your week? Rate your energy 1 to 10. Anything else?" The client fills it out, the coach skims it, and nothing in the program changes.

A check-in is only useful if it can change a decision. If none of the answers would make you adjust anything, you're collecting data for the sake of it, and clients figure that out fast. They stop filling it in, and you lose the one window you had into the six days you don't see them.

Here's how I think about building one.

## Start with the decisions, then write the questions

Before you write a single question, list the decisions you make for a client between sessions. For most coaches it's some version of these:

1. Do we progress the plan, hold it, or pull it back?
2. Is the client actually doing the plan as written?
3. Is something outside the gym getting in the way?
4. Is the goal still the right goal?

Every question on your check-in should feed one of those. If it doesn't, cut it.

## The questions that earn their place

I've written before about [the four things I check before I touch a client's program](/notes/your-client-didnt-plateau-their-week-did): attendance, sleep, life load and effort. Those are the core of a useful weekly check-in, because they tell you whether a stall is the program's fault or the week's.

Beyond that, keep it short:

- **What got done.** Sessions completed as written, not "how training went." A number is easier to act on than a mood.
- **What felt off.** Any lift, position or session that didn't feel right. This is where pain, technique problems and bad exercise fits show up early.
- **What's coming.** Travel, deadlines, a busy stretch at work. This lets you plan around the next week instead of reacting to it.
- **One open question.** "Anything you want me to know?" Some of the most useful things clients tell you come from here.

That's a check-in a client can finish in a couple of minutes. Long forms get rushed or skipped.

## Match the cadence to the client

How often you check in should follow how much the client needs, not a template.

A newer client, or someone in a demanding phase, usually needs a weekly check-in. Things change fast and small problems compound. A consistent, self-directed client might only need a short check-in every week and a deeper review once a month.

I match coaching intensity to the person across everything I do, using an intervention hierarchy: Inadequate, Adequate, Optimal and Enhanced. Check-ins follow the same rule. Use the lowest level of complexity that reliably works. A client who's doing great on a simple weekly check-in doesn't need a longer form to make your coaching look thorough.

## Do something with every answer

This is where most check-ins fail. The client answers, and the answer disappears.

Every check-in should end with one of three outcomes, and the client should hear which one:

1. **Keep going.** Nothing needs to change. Tell them that, and tell them why. "Your sessions are all in and your top sets moved. Same plan this week."
2. **Adjust.** Something changes, and you say what and why. "Sleep's been short all week, so we're cutting the last two sets on squat and keeping the rest."
3. **Talk.** Something needs a conversation, not a form. Pain that keeps coming back, a goal that's shifted, a pattern of missed sessions. Pick up the phone or bring it into the next session.

When clients see their answers turn into decisions, they keep answering honestly. When they don't, the check-in turns into homework.

## Write it down

Keep a running record of what the client said and what you decided. It takes two minutes a week, and it pays off when a client stalls six weeks later and you need to see what changed. It also makes a handoff to another coach far cleaner, because the next coach can see the pattern instead of starting from zero.

Build your check-in around the decisions you need to make, keep it short enough to get finished, and make sure every answer goes somewhere.
