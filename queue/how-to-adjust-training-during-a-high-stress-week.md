Some weeks are just heavy. Work piles up, sleep gets short, family needs you, and the holidays land on top of everything. The question people ask me is whether they should push through and train like normal, or skip it until things calm down.

Usually neither. The best move in a high-stress week is to keep training, but train less.

## Stress doesn't care where it comes from

Your body has one recovery system. Hard training uses it, and so does a stressful week. When life stress goes up, you recover more slowly from the same workout.

Researchers have measured this. In two studies from the same group, people reporting higher stress recovered muscle strength more slowly after a hard leg workout, both in the first hour and over the following four days.[1][2]

So the session that felt fine two weeks ago can dig you a hole this week. Pushing the full plan through a hard week often turns one bad week into three.

## What to keep

**Keep the habit.** The most important thing you have is the routine of showing up. A shorter session on your normal days protects that. Missing the whole week makes the next one harder to start.

**Keep your main lifts.** If your program has one or two key lifts, do those first. They carry most of your progress, and a few good sets are enough to hold on to what you've built.

**Keep the weight, cut the work.** In a hard week I'd rather see someone do fewer sets at a familiar weight than force every set at a lower weight. The goal this week is to hold what you have.

## What to cut

**Cut volume first.** Drop a set or two from each exercise and remove most of the extra work at the end of the session. For most people that's the single best adjustment, because it keeps the quality of the training and lowers the cost.

**Cut the hardest conditioning.** All-out intervals and long, punishing finishers take a lot to recover from. Swap them for an easy walk or bike, or leave them out.

**Cut the expectation of a PR.** This isn't the week to test anything. A clean session at a weight you know is a win.

## What to skip

**Skip sessions you'd have to force.** If you have three training days but realistically only time and energy for two, plan two. A planned two-day week is better than a three-day plan where you miss one and feel behind.

**Skip anything that hurts.** Stress can make aches louder. If something actually hurts, stop that movement and choose something that doesn't. Pain that keeps coming back needs a proper look from the right professional.

## What a stress week can look like

Say you normally train three days a week with four exercises a session, three or four sets each. In a hard week, that might become:

1. Two sessions instead of three.
2. Your two main lifts each session, two or three good sets at a familiar weight.
3. One accessory exercise if you have time, or none.
4. A walk on the days you'd normally train.

That's thirty or forty minutes, twice. It's enough to keep your strength and your routine, and it doesn't take much from a week that's already short on recovery.

## Get back to the plan after

When the week settles down, go back to your normal program. You don't need to make up the missed work, and you don't need to start over. If you trained less but kept showing up, you'll usually pick up right where you were.

If hard weeks keep happening one after another, that's a different problem. At that point the plan needs to change to fit your life, not the other way around. I'd rather build someone a program they can keep through a busy season than one that only works when life is calm.
