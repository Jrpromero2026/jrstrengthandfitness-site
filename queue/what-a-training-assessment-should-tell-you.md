A good assessment should change your plan. If you spend an hour answering questions and moving through tests, and then get the same program everyone else gets, you were tested for show.

Plenty of people come out of an assessment with a list of observations and no idea what any of them mean for their training.

## What you should walk out with

**Where you're starting.** Your goals, your training history, what's gotten in the way before, how you move and how much time you really have. A good coach can sum this up in a couple of sentences, and you should recognize yourself in them.

**What the first block is for.** Every plan has a priority. Getting comfortable under a barbell, building a training habit, getting strong again after a layoff, or chasing a specific number. You should know which one it is for you.

**Why the plan looks the way it does.** The findings should show up in the program. If you couldn't control depth on a free squat, you might start with a box squat and build toward the full movement. If your schedule only holds two days, the plan should be built for two days. Ask your coach to connect one finding to one decision. They should be able to do it on the spot.

**What gets checked again, and when.** An assessment is a starting line. You should know what will be retested and roughly when, so you can see whether the plan is working.

## What an assessment can't tell you

**It isn't a diagnosis.** A trainer can notice that something hurts or that a position looks limited. Figuring out why is a medical job. If something hurts, a good coach sends you to the right professional.

**It isn't a prediction.** Movement screens are useful for choosing where to start. They're poor at predicting who gets hurt. A 2017 meta-analysis of the Functional Movement Screen found the link between scores and later injury wasn't strong enough to support using it as an injury prediction tool.[1] A low score tells your coach what to work on first.

**It isn't your ceiling.** One awkward squat on day one means the squat needs coaching. Most early movement problems clean up with practice and the right variation. The first few weeks of training will tell your coach more than any single test, which is why the assessment should keep going after the first session.

## Questions to ask afterward

If you've been assessed and you're not sure what came out of it, ask:

- What did you find that changed my program?
- What is the first block supposed to improve?
- When do we retest, and what are we retesting?
- What would make you refer me to someone else?
