Passing your certification exam means you know enough to start. Most of what clients actually pay for gets learned after it.

An exam can test what you know about anatomy, program design and safety. It can't test whether you can run a first conversation, write a plan for the person in front of you, coach a squat in real time or keep a client past their first month. Those are the skills that decide whether you have a career.

## What to work on in year one

**The first conversation.** Goals, history, schedule, what's gotten in the way before and what the client actually enjoys. If you get this right, half the programming decisions make themselves. I covered the questions that run it in [5 Questions to Ask During a Personal Training Consultation](/notes/5-questions-to-ask-during-a-personal-training-consultation).

**Programming for one person.** The textbook gives you principles. Your client gives you two available days, a bad shoulder and a goal of feeling strong again. Learn to connect what you find to what you write. I wrote about that in [how to turn an assessment into programming decisions](/notes/how-to-turn-an-assessment-into-programming-decisions).

**Coaching a few lifts really well.** You don't need a thousand exercises. You need to coach a squat, a hinge, a push, a pull and a carry so well that you can fix the most common problems with one cue. Pick the one thing that matters most, fix that and move on.

**Keeping clients through the first month.** The first five sessions decide whether a client stays. Don't spend them impressing anyone with intensity. If a new client starts missing sessions, act before the third miss. See [when a new client misses their first two sessions](/notes/when-a-new-client-misses-their-first-two-sessions).

**Writing things down.** What you found, what you decided and why, and what happened. A short record for every client keeps your programming honest and makes you better faster, because you can see your own patterns. Start with [what a useful client check-in should include](/notes/what-a-useful-client-check-in-includes).

## Keep it simple

New trainers tend to over-program. They've just learned a lot and want to use all of it. Use the lowest level of complexity that reliably works. Complexity is earned by the level beneath it. Most coaches over-intervene, and new coaches do it most.

A beginner client needs a safe, simple plan and a reason to keep coming back. Give them that well and you'll have more clients than the trainer with the cleverest program.

## Choose continuing education on purpose

After the exam, it's easy to collect courses. Before you pay for the next one, ask whether it will change how you coach the clients you have now. I wrote about [what makes a certification credible](/notes/what-makes-personal-trainer-certification-credible), and the same questions apply to anything you take next.

## Find people better than you

If you can, work around experienced coaches. Watch how they run a first session, how they cue and how they handle a client who's struggling. Ask them to watch you and tell you what they'd change. A year of honest feedback from a good coach is worth more than most courses.
